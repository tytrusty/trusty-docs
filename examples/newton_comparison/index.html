Comparison with Newton¶

This page compares TrustySim with Newton, NVIDIA's open source physics library, on a handful of small scenes that each isolate one behavior. We are not looking at performance here and are showing instead the effects that each simulator captures, so in every scene the Newton-VBD video (Newton's Vertex Block Descent solver, which runs a fixed number of iterations per step) comes first, on the left or above, and the TrustySim video, which solves every step to convergence, comes second, on the right or below, with Newton at commit b6b62318.

Resolution and iteration count¶

We start with the simplest case, a beam clamped at one end and released under gravity, simulated at increasing resolution with the material held fixed. A converged solver should give the same motion at every resolution, which is what TrustySim does, whereas with a fixed number of iterations Newton-VBD behaves like a softer and softer material as we increase the resolution, since each iteration only passes information between neighboring vertices and so the same number of iterations resolves less and less of the beam. More iterations bring it back, but the number needed grows with the resolution, so it is a setting that has to be tuned again whenever the mesh changes, and we will see the same effect in many of the scenes below.

Inversion-free materials¶

Next we crush a soft cube. Newton-VBD currently supports only invertible material models such as stable neo-Hookean, which stay finite when an element turns inside out, while TrustySim also supports inversion-free models such as neo-Hookean, which is what we use here. This may seem like a subtle difference, but it has a big effect in large deformations, and under compression Newton-VBD gives a non-physical deformation, with the upper part of the cube shearing over the lower part, instead of the bulging at the sides that we see in TrustySim.

Twisting¶

Twisting the cube shows the same difference, with Newton-VBD pinching the cube into a narrow neck, while in TrustySim all contact, including the self contact as the cube folds, is resolved with no penetration.

Contact stiffness¶

Newton also has a compliant contact, which requires the user to tune the stiffness of the contact in order to avoid intersections, and here the vertices that pass through the sheet are drawn in red. The stiffness that is needed varies with the dynamics in the scene and with the materials, which makes it an unintuitive thing to tune, whereas with our IPC-based contact model there is nothing to tune in order to guarantee a penetration-free result. Newton-VBD has since added Divide-and-Truncate (DAT), which limits how far vertices can move at a contact, but it still needs a bound on how far things move between collision detections, which depends on the speeds in the scene and is another parameter the user has to set, and the videos here do not use it.

Several settings at once¶

This scene is more of an integration test, a stiff bridge made of a stiff plank on two supports with a heavy box at its middle, and it shows that you are rarely tuning one parameter in isolation. The contact stiffness, the resolution and the iteration count all interact in Newton-VBD, and changing any of them has a dramatic effect on the result, so that in none of these four runs does the plank stay on its supports, while TrustySim keeps it there at both resolutions.

How far a push travels¶

Here a bar is pushed into a wall, and the color shows how compressed each part of it is. With a fixed number of iterations Newton-VBD shows its resolution dependence directly, since you can see how quickly the deformation travels along the bar, which is slower on the finer mesh, whereas TrustySim solves every step to convergence so this speed is not a factor and the bar compresses evenly at both resolutions.

Buckling¶

The same effect shows up when buckling a slender column. Not resolving the deformation within a time step makes the elastic response lag behind the motion over several steps, so Newton-VBD hardly deflects until the end of the push and its motion looks damped, while TrustySim follows Euler's buckling theory closely and ends in almost the same shape on both meshes.

Fabric stretch¶

The cloth lift is much the same, with an elasticity that depends on the resolution, so that for a fixed fabric stiffness the finer cloth stretches far more in Newton-VBD, while TrustySim stretches by the same small amount at both resolutions. TrustySim can also cap the stretch with strain limiting, which Newton-VBD does not support, and we leave it off here so that the stretch comes from the fabric alone.

A cable in a duct¶

Our last scene is a cable pushed along a frictional duct, shown at the lowest and the highest resolution with Newton-VBD above and TrustySim below. This should not depend on the resolution either, and in TrustySim it does not, while in Newton-VBD the finer cable buckles inside the duct, which is an unwanted result in a very benign example.
